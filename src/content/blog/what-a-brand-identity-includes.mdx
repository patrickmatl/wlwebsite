**A brand identity is the logo plus everything that makes the rest of your material look like it came from the same business: a logo suite in several versions, a colour palette with exact values for print and screen, typefaces, an imagery or illustration style, a tone of voice, stationery, templates and the usage rules that hold it together.** A brand guidelines document is where those rules are written down so that someone who has never met you can apply them correctly.

That is the short answer. The longer one matters because "branding" is sold in very different sizes. One studio's "branding package" is a logo and a business card; another's is a hundred-page manual. Knowing what the parts are, and which of them your business actually needs, is the easiest way to compare quotes and avoid paying for a shelf ornament.

## The parts of a brand identity

### 1. The logo suite, not just "the logo"

A single logo file is not enough, because the logo has to sit on surfaces that disagree with each other. A working logo suite usually includes:

- **The primary logo**, the version you use whenever there is room for it.
- **A secondary or stacked version** for spaces that are tall rather than wide, such as a social media profile picture or the side of a vehicle.
- **A symbol or icon on its own**, if the design has one, for favicons, app icons and embroidery on a shirt pocket.
- **Colour variants**: full colour, one colour, and reversed (white or light) for dark backgrounds and photographs.

Each of these should exist as vector artwork for print and signage, and as PNG files for screens. If you are not sure what the difference is, our guide to [logo file formats](/creative-industry-blog-pretoria/logo-file-formats-explained) explains which file goes to the printer, the signwriter and the web developer.

### 2. A colour palette with actual values

"Our colours are navy and gold" is not a palette. A palette names each colour and gives its values in the systems your suppliers use: CMYK for printed work, RGB and HEX for screens, and often a Pantone reference where a printer has to match a colour exactly, for example on corporate gifts or signage. Without those numbers, the same navy comes back from three suppliers as three different navies, and nobody is technically wrong.

A good palette also says how the colours are used. Which is the main colour? Which is an accent, used sparingly? Which colours may be used for text, and which are too light to read on white? Two lines of guidance here prevent most of the visual drift you see in small businesses.

### 3. Typography

Typography is the most underestimated part of an identity. Choose a heading typeface and a body typeface, the weights you use, and how headings, subheadings and body text relate in size. Then add the practical part: which system font to use in Word, Google Docs and email, where the brand fonts are not installed. Most of a business's documents are typed by staff, not designers, so the fallback font is the one your clients will see most often.

### 4. Imagery and illustration style

If you use photography, the identity should say what kind: people or products, natural light or studio, real staff or stock, colour or black and white. If you use illustration or icons, it should define the line weight, the level of detail and the colours allowed. This is what makes a social feed or a company profile look intentional rather than assembled from whatever was available that week.

Many identities also include **graphic devices**: patterns, shapes or line work drawn from the logo. They give designers something to work with on backgrounds, packaging, the back of business cards and exhibition stands, without repeating the logo everywhere.

### 5. Tone of voice

How the business sounds is part of how it looks. A tone-of-voice section does not need to be long. It sets out three or four traits ("plain-spoken, not casual", "confident, not boastful"), a few words you use and a few you avoid, and short before-and-after examples. It matters most when several people write on your behalf: sales staff, a social media manager, whoever answers the WhatsApp line.

### 6. Stationery

The everyday documents that carry the brand to clients: letterhead, business cards, email signatures, and often a compliments slip, envelope or presentation folder. These are where an identity first proves whether it works at small sizes and in black and white on an office printer.

### 7. Templates

Templates are the part of a brand that saves the most money over time, because they stop staff rebuilding documents from scratch. Typical templates are a PowerPoint or Google Slides deck, a proposal or report cover page, quote and invoice layouts, and social media post templates. A business that sends tenders or proposals every month will feel the benefit of these long before it feels the benefit of a new logo.

### 8. Usage rules

The rules that protect the logo and the system around it:

- **Clear space**: the minimum empty area around the logo, usually measured using a part of the logo itself so it scales.
- **Minimum size**: the smallest the logo can be printed or shown before details close up.
- **Backgrounds**: which version goes on light, dark and photographic backgrounds.
- **Misuse examples**: no stretching, no recolouring, no drop shadows, no outlines, no placing it on a busy photograph. Showing the wrong versions is more effective than describing the right one.
- **Co-branding**: how your logo sits next to a partner's, a sponsor's or a parent organisation's.

## What a brand guidelines document should cover

A brand guidelines document (also called a brand manual or style guide) gathers all of the above into one reference. Length is not a measure of quality. A clear twelve-page PDF that a printer actually reads is worth more than a manual nobody opens. A useful document covers:

1. **A short introduction**: what the business does and the impression the brand should give, in a paragraph.
2. **The logo suite**, with every version shown and labelled.
3. **Clear space, minimum sizes and background rules.**
4. **Misuse examples**, shown visually.
5. **The colour palette** with CMYK, RGB, HEX and, where relevant, Pantone values, plus how the colours are weighted.
6. **Typography**, including fallback fonts for everyday software.
7. **Imagery, illustration and graphic devices**, with examples.
8. **Tone of voice**, with examples.
9. **Applications**: the stationery, templates, signage and social layouts shown in use.
10. **Where to get the files and who to contact**, so a supplier knows where the master artwork lives.

The last point is the one most often missing, and it causes most of the problems. A guidelines document is only useful if the files it describes are organised, named sensibly and handed over with it.

## When a logo alone is enough

Not every business needs the full system on day one. A logo with properly prepared files is often enough when:

- you are a sole trader or a very small team, and you produce almost all your material yourself;
- you are testing an idea and expect the business to change shape within a year or two;
- your customers mostly find you through referrals and rarely see printed material.

In that case, buy a good logo with the colour versions and file formats you need, and write down the colour values and font names yourself. You can build the rest of the identity later around the same mark. Our [logo design service](/logo-design-pretoria) is set up for exactly this.

## When you need the full identity

You need the full system when other people are producing material in your name. The signs are familiar:

- more than a handful of staff create documents, presentations or quotes;
- you submit tenders, proposals or company profiles where looking organised is part of being taken seriously;
- you use outside suppliers for signage, printing, vehicle branding, uniforms or your website;
- you sell a product off a shelf, where packaging has to carry the brand on its own;
- you have noticed that your logo looks slightly different on every item you own.

## Rebrand or refresh?

If you already have an identity, the question is usually not "new brand or nothing" but how much to change.

**A refresh** keeps the name and the recognisable core of the logo, and fixes what is wrong around it: an outdated typeface, a colour that prints badly, detail that disappears at small sizes, or the simple fact that nobody has a usable file. Customers should notice that you look sharper, not that you have changed. A refresh is right for most established businesses, because recognition built over years is worth keeping. Sometimes the cheapest refresh is just a vector redraw of the existing logo, which we do for R1,040.

**A rebrand** changes the identity at its root: new mark, sometimes a new name. It is the right call when the business has genuinely changed: a merger, a move into a new market, a name that clashes with another company's, or a brand that describes what you did a decade ago rather than what you do now. A rebrand should start with the questions about who you serve and how you want to be seen, not with a logo sketch.

Whichever you choose, plan the changeover. List every place the old identity appears (signage, vehicles, uniforms, document templates, email signatures, website, Google Business Profile, social accounts, tender documents) and decide what changes on launch day and what is replaced as it wears out. Digital items are cheap to switch at once; physical items can usually wait for their next reprint.

## How to use this list when comparing quotes

When two studios quote very different amounts for "branding", put their proposals next to the eight parts above and tick off what each one actually includes. Check how many concepts you will see, how many rounds of changes are included, which logo versions and file formats you receive, whether colour values and fonts are documented, and whether you own the final files. Those details explain most of the price difference, and they are what you will be living with long after the launch.
