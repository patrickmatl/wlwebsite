**Before a website build starts, a South African business should have ten things in hand: a domain registered in its own name, a decision on hosting, the written copy, usable photos, a page list, a plan for POPIA on forms and cookies, a Google Business Profile, analytics access, an agreement on who owns what at handover, and a plan for maintenance.** None of them needs technical knowledge. All of them affect how long the project takes and how much control you have over the site afterwards.

We have built websites for Pretoria businesses since 2013, and the projects that run late almost never run late because of design or code. They stall because the copy is still being written, nobody can find the domain login, or a decision about the services page is waiting for a partner who is away. Work through this list first, and the build itself becomes the quick part.

## 1. Your domain name, registered in your name

Your domain (for example `yourbusiness.co.za`) is your address on the internet and the part of your email addresses after the @. It is also the one thing that is hardest to get back if it is registered to the wrong person.

**What to check:**

- **Who is the registrant?** The registrant is the legal owner. It should be your business or you personally, never your web designer, IT contractor or a former employee. Ask your registrar, or look up the domain on the registry's public lookup service.
- **Can you log in?** You need access to the account at the registrar where the domain is managed. If an old developer registered it, ask for it to be transferred to an account in your name before the new build starts. Start early, as transfers take time.
- **Is it set to renew?** Domains are renewed, usually yearly. If the renewal notice goes to an old email address and the domain lapses, your website and email stop working, and someone else can register it.

**A note on .co.za:** the .co.za space is run by the ZA Central Registry, and you register a name through an accredited registrar, which is often your hosting company. Fees are modest and vary between registrars, so compare a few. Most South African businesses are well served by a .co.za, because it tells local customers you trade here. If you also want the .com, register it at the same time and point it at the same site.

## 2. Hosting

Hosting is the server space where your website lives. It is a separate cost from the build, paid monthly or yearly to the hosting company.

**What to decide:**

- **New or existing hosting?** If you already have a hosting account, find out which plan it is, who holds the login, and whether your email runs on it. Plan any email move carefully; it is the part most likely to go wrong.
- **Whose name is the account in?** Same rule as the domain: the account should be in your business's name, with your billing details. Your developer can be given access, but should not be the account holder.
- **What does the site need?** A three-page business site needs very little. An online shop with hundreds of products, or a site expecting traffic spikes from advertising, needs more. A server located in South Africa can help load times for local visitors.
- **Is an SSL certificate included?** Every site should run on HTTPS. Most hosts now include a free certificate, but confirm it.

## 3. Content and copy

This is the item that sets the timeline more than any other. Design can only be finalised once the real words are in place, because a heading of four words and a heading of fourteen need different layouts.

**For each page, have ready:**

- A clear heading that says what the page is about in plain language.
- The main text. For a services page, explain what you do, who it is for, how it works and what happens next.
- The questions customers always ask you. These make useful FAQ sections and are often what people type into Google.
- Contact details exactly as they should appear everywhere: business name, street address, phone number and email.

If writing is not your strength, that is normal. You can hire a copywriter, ask your designer whether copywriting is offered, or write rough notes and have them edited. What does not work is starting the design with placeholder text and hoping the words will arrive later. That is the single most common reason a two-week project becomes a two-month one.

## 4. Photos and visual material

Real photos of your team, premises, products and completed work do more for trust than any stock image. Before the build:

- **Gather what you have.** Logo files (ideally a vector version such as AI, EPS, SVG or PDF), brand colours and fonts, and any existing photography.
- **Check the quality.** Photos for a website should be sharp and reasonably large. Images saved from WhatsApp or social media are usually compressed too much to use well.
- **Check you have the rights.** Only use photos you took, paid a photographer for, or licensed properly. Images copied from Google search are not free to use.
- **Fill the gaps.** If you have no usable photos, a short professional shoot of your team and premises is often worth more than an extra page on the site.

## 5. Your page list (sitemap)

A sitemap at this stage simply means the list of pages and how they connect. It decides the scope of the project, and on most fixed-price packages it decides the price.

A typical small business site looks like this:

- Home
- About
- Services (or one page per main service)
- Contact

Larger sites add separate service pages, a team page, a gallery or portfolio, pages for the areas you serve, and a blog. If customers search for a particular service by name, it probably deserves its own page.

Write the list down, note the purpose of each page in one sentence, and mark which pages are essential for launch and which can come later.

## 6. POPIA basics for forms and cookies

The Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA) governs how South African organisations collect and use personal information. A website touches it in a few predictable places. What follows is general information, not legal advice; if your business handles sensitive information, such as health or financial details, speak to a legal adviser.

**Where a website collects personal information:**

- **Contact and quote forms** collect names, phone numbers and email addresses.
- **Newsletter sign-ups** collect email addresses for marketing.
- **Analytics and advertising tools** may set cookies that track how individual visitors use the site.

**What most business websites should have:**

- **A privacy policy** that explains what information you collect, why, how long you keep it, who you share it with, and how people can ask to see or delete it.
- **A short notice on each form** saying what the information will be used for, with a link to the privacy policy.
- **Clear consent for marketing.** Do not tick a newsletter box for people by default. Under POPIA, electronic direct marketing to people who are not already your customers generally needs their consent first.
- **A cookie banner** where the site uses non-essential cookies, such as analytics or advertising, so visitors know what is being set and can make a choice.
- **An information officer.** Every business has one under POPIA, by default the head of the business. Check whether yours has been registered with the Information Regulator.

## 7. Google Business Profile

For a local business, your Google Business Profile is often seen before your website. It is the panel with your address, hours, reviews and map pin that appears when someone searches your name or a service near them.

- **Claim and verify it** if you have not already. It is free to set up.
- **Make the details match your website exactly.** The same business name, address format and phone number on your profile, your website and directory listings helps Google trust that they all describe the same business.
- **Choose accurate categories** and add real photos.
- **Link to the right page.** Usually the home page, or a location page if you have several branches.

## 8. Analytics and Search Console

Without analytics you cannot tell whether the website is doing its job. Before the build, decide who in the business will look at the numbers, and make sure that person owns the accounts.

- **Google Analytics** shows how many people visit, where they come from and what they do on the site. Create the account under a business Google login, not a designer's personal one, and give your developer access.
- **Google Search Console** shows which searches your site appears for and flags technical problems Google finds. It is free and worth setting up on every site.
- **Decide what counts as a result.** Usually form submissions, phone taps and email clicks. Ask your designer to set these up as tracked events so you can see enquiries, not just visits.

## 9. Who owns what at handover

Agree on this in writing before the project starts. It saves real money if you change suppliers later.

At the end of the project you should have:

- **Administrator logins** for the website itself.
- **The domain and hosting** in your name, with you as account holder.
- **Analytics and Search Console** under your business's Google account.
- **Ownership of the content**: the copy you supplied or paid for, your photos and your logo.
- **Clarity on licences**: if the site uses paid themes, plugins or fonts, know whose name the licence is in and when it renews.
- **A short handover note** listing where everything lives and who to call.

A reputable studio will not object to any of this. Be wary of an arrangement where the developer keeps the domain, the hosting and the only admin login, and a monthly fee is the only way to keep the site online.

## 10. Maintenance after launch

A website built on a content management system needs updates. The core software, themes and plugins release new versions, some of them fixing security problems. Backups need to run, and someone should notice if the contact form quietly stops sending.

You have three options:

- **Do it yourself.** Fine for a simple site if someone in the business is comfortable logging in monthly to apply updates and check things still work.
- **Pay per job.** Call your developer when something needs changing. Nobody is watching for problems in between.
- **A maintenance plan.** A fixed monthly fee covers updates, backups and security monitoring, and usually some content changes. Our [website maintenance plans](/pricing/website-maintenance-pretoria) work this way.

Whichever you choose, decide before launch day. The worst option is the common default: nobody is responsible, and the site goes two years without an update.

## The checklist in short

Before your first meeting with a web designer, aim to have:

1. Domain registered in your name, with the login to hand.
2. Hosting chosen, or details of your existing account and email set-up.
3. Copy written or commissioned for every launch page.
4. Logo files, brand colours and usable photos.
5. A page list with a one-line purpose for each page.
6. A plan for POPIA: privacy policy, form notices, marketing consent and cookies.
7. Google Business Profile claimed and verified.
8. A business Google account for Analytics and Search Console.
9. Ownership and handover agreed in writing.
10. A maintenance decision.

You do not need every item finished before you call a studio, but the more you can tick off, the faster and cheaper your project will be.
