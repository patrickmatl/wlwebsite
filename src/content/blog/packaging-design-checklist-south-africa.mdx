**Good packaging starts long before anyone opens a design program.** Many packaging delays and reprints come from missing information: a label size nobody confirmed with the printer, a barcode that arrived after the artwork was signed off, an allergen line added at the last minute to a back panel that had no room for it. This checklist covers what to gather, what to plan space for and what to ask your printer, so your first print run is the right one.

WL CreationX has worked from its Pretoria studio since 2013, and the list below is the one we work through with every [packaging design](/pricing/packaging-design-pretoria) client, written so you can use it whoever designs your pack.

## 1. Gather the product facts

Before the brief, write down the basics in one document. It sounds obvious, but it is easy to skip.

- **Product name and descriptor.** The brand name, the product name and a plain description of what it is ("chilli sauce", "hand cream", "rooibos tea bags"). A shopper who has never heard of your brand needs the descriptor.
- **Variants.** Every flavour, scent, size and colour you sell now, and the ones you plan to add in the next year. Planning for them now is far cheaper than redesigning later.
- **Net quantity.** The exact weight or volume as it will be declared on the pack.
- **All the text.** Ingredients, directions for use, storage instructions, warnings, marketing claims, your contact details. Send final, checked copy. Changing an ingredient list after the artwork is finished costs time and introduces errors.
- **Where it will be sold.** Markets, farm stalls, independent shops, pharmacies, chain stores, online, or export. Each channel changes what the pack needs.
- **Who it competes with.** Photograph the shelf where your product will sit. The design has to stand out against those packs, not against a white background on a screen.

## 2. Get the dieline from your printer

A dieline is the technical drawing of your pack laid flat: the outline, the folds, the glue flaps, the cut lines. For labels it is the exact label size, corner radius and the area that can safely carry text.

**The dieline must come from your printer or packaging supplier, not from a template on the internet.** It has to match their cutting tools and the actual container you are buying. A jar from one supplier can differ from a near-identical jar from another by enough to make a label wrinkle or overlap.

Ask your printer for:

- the dieline as a vector file (usually PDF or AI);
- the bleed they need (commonly about 3 mm) and the safe zone for text;
- for roll labels, the unwind direction if the labels will be applied by a machine;
- the minimum text size, line weight and reverse-text size they can print cleanly on your material;
- their preferred file format and colour profile.

If you have not chosen a printer yet, choose one before the design is finalised. Concepts can start without a dieline, but final artwork cannot.

## 3. Plan space for the label information

Every product category has information that must appear on the pack, and much of it lives on the back and side panels. A common mistake is to design a beautiful front panel and then try to squeeze the required information into whatever is left.

Plan space from the first concept for the information that applies to your product. In general terms this can include:

- **Product name and what the product is**, clearly on the main panel.
- **Net quantity**, usually on the main panel.
- **Ingredients and allergens** for food, and ingredient lists for many cosmetic and personal care products.
- **Nutritional information** for many food products, often in a set table format.
- **Manufacturer, packer, importer or distributor details**, such as a name and a physical address.
- **Country of origin** where it applies.
- **Directions for use, storage instructions and warnings.**
- **A barcode** if you sell through retailers.
- **Batch number and date areas** (best before, use by or expiry) that the printer leaves blank so you can print or stamp them at packing.

**Confirm the rules for your specific product.** South Africa has labelling requirements for different categories (food labelling rules for foodstuffs, separate requirements for cosmetics, medicines, agricultural products and others), and the Consumer Protection Act also deals with product labelling and information. The details depend on exactly what you sell and how you describe it, and they change over time. Check the requirements for your category with the relevant authority or a compliance adviser before you sign off artwork. A designer can lay the information out clearly and legibly, but should not be the one deciding what the law requires on your pack.

One practical tip: be careful with claims. Words like "natural", "organic", "sugar-free" or health claims can bring extra requirements. If a claim is going on the pack, check it with your adviser at the same time as the rest of the label.

## 4. Sort out barcodes early

If you plan to sell through retailers, you will almost certainly need a barcode. In South Africa, barcode numbers are issued by **GS1 South Africa**, which allocates company prefixes and numbers to members. Apply early: your barcode number has to be in the artwork before it goes to print, and registration is not always instant.

When the barcode goes onto the design:

- Use one number per product variant and size. A 250 ml and a 500 ml bottle of the same sauce need different barcodes.
- Place it as vector artwork from a proper barcode generator, never a screenshot or a copied image.
- Keep the bars dark on a light background. Black on white is safest. Red bars, and bars on red or some metallic backgrounds, can fail to scan.
- Leave the clear margin (the quiet zone) around it empty.
- Do not shrink it below the minimum size or cut its height without checking the GS1 guidance and your printer.
- Keep it off folds, seams and tight curves, where it can distort.
- Scan the printed proof before approving the run.

Retailers may have their own supplier requirements for barcodes and packaging. If you are working towards a listing, ask the buyer for the supplier manual early.

## 5. Get colour right

What you see on screen is not what will print. The same file can look noticeably different on two printers or two materials.

- **CMYK** is the standard four-colour process for full-colour printing. Your artwork should be set up in CMYK using the profile your printer supplies.
- **Pantone (spot) colours** are pre-mixed inks used when a brand colour must match exactly every time. If your brand colour is critical, ask your printer whether the job will run with a spot colour or as CMYK, because some colours (bright oranges, greens and blues especially) cannot be matched closely in CMYK.
- **Material changes colour.** Ink on kraft paper looks darker and warmer than on white stock. On clear or metallic film, colours need a white ink layer underneath to look solid, and that white layer has to be set up in the artwork.
- **Ask for a proof.** A digital PDF proof checks layout and text. Only a printed proof on the real material shows you the colour. For a large run, it is worth paying for.

## 6. Choose materials and finishes with the printer

Materials and finishes affect the unit price, durability and how the artwork must be built, so discuss them with your printer early.

**Common materials:**

- **Paper labels** are economical and print well, but can wrinkle or tear when wet.
- **Synthetic labels** (plastic films such as polypropylene) resist water, oil and fridge condensation, which suits sauces, drinks, cosmetics and cleaning products.
- **Clear labels** give a "no label" look on glass or clear plastic, but need a white underprint for solid colour.
- **Board** for folding cartons comes in different thicknesses and coatings. Thicker board feels sturdier but changes how the folds sit.
- **Flexible films** for pouches and sachets are usually laminated layers chosen for the product inside, such as a barrier for coffee or snacks.
- **Kraft** paper and board give a natural look and print with a duller, darker result.

**Common finishes:** gloss or matt lamination, varnishes, spot UV (a raised gloss on selected areas), hot foil, and embossing or debossing. Each one adds cost and usually needs its own layer or separation in the artwork, so decide on them before the artwork is final rather than after.

**Sustainability.** If recyclable or reduced-plastic packaging matters to you, raise it early with your packaging supplier. The material choice is theirs to advise on; the design then works within it, for example by using fewer inks or avoiding laminates where a mono-material pack is the goal.

## 7. Design for the shelf, not the screen

A packaging design is judged in a shop, under shop lighting, next to competitors, often from a metre or two away and for a few seconds.

- **Test at real size.** Print the concept at actual size and stick it on the real container.
- **Step back.** From two metres, can you tell what the product is, whose it is and which variant?
- **Check every face.** Shelves are often stacked side-on, and some products hang from hooks. Make sure the face the shopper actually sees carries the brand.
- **Think about shelf-ready packaging.** Retailers sometimes want outer cases or display trays that go straight onto the shelf. Ask whether that applies before you design.

## 8. Plan ranges for consistency

If you will sell more than one product, design the range as a system rather than one pack at a time.

- Fix a master layout: the logo, product name, descriptor, variant name and key visual always sit in the same positions.
- Give each variant its own colour or marker, chosen so the variants are distinct from each other and from competitors.
- Set a naming pattern ("Smoky Chilli 250 ml", "Garlic Chilli 250 ml") and stick to it.
- Keep the back panel layout identical across the range, so new copy drops into the same structure.

## 9. Work closely with your printer

Treat the printer as part of the team, not the last stop.

- **Choose the print process with them.** Digital printing suits short runs and many variants. Litho, flexo and other processes suit longer runs but have higher set-up costs and minimum quantities.
- **Approve the proof carefully.** Check every word, the barcode, the net quantity, the colours and the cut lines. Once you sign off, mistakes are yours to pay for.
- **Keep a copy of the approved proof** with the date, so the next reprint can be checked against it.

## 10. Plan photography and mockups

Before the first run arrives, 3D mockups let you show the pack to buyers, investors and your online audience. Once you have printed stock, real photographs work better for an online shop and retailer listings. ## The short checklist

Use this as a final check before you brief a designer:

- Product name, descriptor and all variants listed
- Net quantity confirmed for each size
- Final, checked text for every panel
- Label requirements for your product category confirmed with the relevant authority or your compliance adviser
- Barcode numbers from GS1 South Africa (if selling through retailers)
- Printer chosen, dieline and specification sheet received
- Material and finishes discussed with the printer
- Brand assets ready: logo files, colours, fonts
- Photos of the shelf and competitors
- Plans for future variants noted
- Proof process agreed with the printer
