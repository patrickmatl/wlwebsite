**An annual report tells the people an organisation answers to what it set out to do, what it actually did, how it was governed and what its finances look like at year-end.** In South Africa that usually means a set of narrative sections (statements from the chair and chief executive, an overview, strategy and performance, governance, risk and sustainability) followed by the annual financial statements and, for companies, the notice of the annual general meeting.

This guide walks through those sections in the order readers normally meet them, explains integrated reporting and how public entity reports differ, and ends with the part that decides whether a report is finished calmly or at midnight: planning backwards from the audit and board dates.

**Please read this as a design and production guide, not accounting or legal advice.** We are a design studio. What your organisation must disclose, and when, is a question for your auditors, company secretary, legal advisers and, for public bodies, your oversight department. What we can tell you is how reports are usually put together and what makes them easier to produce and to read.

## The usual sections of an annual report

Not every report has every section, and the order varies. But most South African annual reports are built from some version of the following.

### Statements from the chair and the chief executive

The chair's statement looks at the year from the board's point of view: strategy, governance, the external environment and what the board expects next. The chief executive's review is more operational, covering what was achieved, what was not and why. In smaller organisations the two are often combined into a single foreword.

Design note: these are the most-read pages in the report, so keep them short, give them a strong opening spread and resist filling them with photographs of handshakes. A single pull quote and a small set of headline numbers do more work.

### Organisational overview

Who you are, what you do, where you operate and how you are structured. This is usually where an organogram, a map of operations and a "year at a glance" spread live. For a public entity it often includes the mandate, the legislation the entity was established under and its vision and mission.

### Strategy and performance

The heart of the report: the goals you set, the measures you used and how you performed against them. Companies typically report on financial and operational performance by business segment. NPOs report on programmes and the people reached. Public entities report performance information against the targets in their plans.

Design note: performance against targets is easier to read as a table or chart than as prose. Decide early on one consistent way to show "target, actual, achieved or not", and use it everywhere.

### Governance

Board and committee composition, meeting attendance, how the board oversees strategy, risk and ethics, and for many organisations a statement on how they apply the King Code on corporate governance. Listed and larger companies usually include a remuneration report here or straight after it.

Governance sections tend to be text-heavy and written by the company secretary or board secretariat. They deserve the same grid and type as the rest of the report, so they do not read like an appendix bolted on at the end.

### Risk

The principal risks the organisation faces, how they are assessed and what is being done about them. A risk heat map or a simple table of risks, owners and responses is common.

### Sustainability and ESG

Environmental, social and governance information: energy and water use, emissions, health and safety, staff and transformation data, community investment. Some organisations put this in the annual report; others publish a separate sustainability or ESG report and summarise it in the annual report. If yours is a standalone document, our [sustainability and ESG report design](/pricing/sustainability-esg-report-design-services-pretoria) page explains how we approach it.

### Annual financial statements

The statements themselves (financial position, profit or loss and other comprehensive income, changes in equity and cash flows, or the equivalents for your sector), the auditor's report and the directors' or accounting authority's responsibilities and approval. Your accounting framework decides the exact contents; your auditors decide whether they are right.

### Notes to the financial statements

Accounting policies and the detailed notes that support the numbers in the primary statements. In a typical report the notes run to more pages than anything else, and they are where typesetting errors most often hide: a note number referred to in the statement of financial position that no longer matches the note it points to, or a table where a total was edited but a row was not.

### AGM notice and proxy form (for companies)

Companies that hold an annual general meeting often include the notice of the AGM, the resolutions and a form of proxy at the back of the report, or publish them as a separate document sent with it. These pages are short but must match the company secretary's approved text exactly.

## What integrated reporting means

An integrated report is not simply an annual report with a sustainability section added. The idea, set out in the international integrated reporting framework, is to explain how an organisation creates value over time: its business model, the resources and relationships it depends on (often described as the "capitals": financial, manufactured, intellectual, human, social and relationship, and natural), the risks and opportunities it faces, its strategy, and how its performance connects to all of that.

South Africa was an early adopter of integrated reporting, largely because the King Code on corporate governance has long encouraged organisations to think and report in an integrated way. Many listed companies and some public entities now publish an integrated report, often with the full annual financial statements as a separate document.

For design, integrated reporting changes three things:

- **Structure.** The report is organised around value creation and material matters rather than around departments.
- **Connections.** Readers should be able to follow a thread from a risk to the strategy that responds to it to the performance measure that tracks it. Consistent icons, colours and cross-references make that possible.
- **Length discipline.** Integrated reports are meant to be concise. Detail moves to supplementary reports and the website.

## Annual reports for public entities

Many public entities, including national and provincial entities and state-owned companies, report under the Public Finance Management Act (PFMA). Their annual reports typically combine performance information, which sets out what the entity achieved against the targets in its plans, with governance information, human resource information and the audited annual financial statements and the auditor's report.

National Treasury and oversight departments issue guidance on the structure and content of these reports, and that guidance is updated from time to time, so work from the current version your entity has been given. The order of sections is often prescribed, which means the design job is different from a corporate report: rather than inventing a structure, you are making a fixed structure clear.

Good design still helps within those limits:

- A clear table style for performance information, showing each target, the actual result and the reason for any deviation in the same place every time.
- An organogram that can be read at the size it is printed.
- The auditor's report reproduced exactly as issued.
- A screen and accessible version, because members of the public, journalists and committee researchers mostly read these reports online.

## Make it accessible

A growing share of annual report readers never see the printed copy. They open a PDF on a laptop or phone, and some use screen readers or other assistive technology. An accessible PDF:

- is **tagged**, so headings, paragraphs, lists and tables are identified as such;
- has a **logical reading order**, so text is read in the order it is meant to be, not the order it was placed on the page;
- has **bookmarks** and a working contents page;
- sets the **document language**;
- gives charts and photographs **alternative text** that describes what they show; and
- uses **real text**, not images of text, with enough colour contrast to be legible.

Plan this from the start. Retro-fitting accessibility onto a finished file takes far longer than building it in, and the alternative text for charts should be written or approved by someone who understands the data.

## Print or online, or both

Fewer organisations now print large runs of their annual report. Common patterns are:

- **Online first, short print run.** A screen PDF and accessible PDF on the website, and a small number of printed copies for the board, the AGM and key stakeholders.
- **Interactive edition.** An [interactive PDF](/pricing/interactive-digital-publication-interactive-pdf-design-pretoria) with a clickable contents page and internal links, useful for long reports read on screen.
- **Full print.** Still common where reports are handed out at events, posted to members or kept on record in printed form.

Decide early, because it affects the design. A report designed mainly for screens can use landscape pages, larger type and fewer columns; a printed report needs a page count that suits the binding, and paper and finishing decisions that affect both cost and schedule.

## Plan backwards from your audit and board dates

The single most useful thing you can do is build the schedule backwards. Start with the dates that cannot move and work back to today:

1. **The publication, AGM, submission or tabling date.** The last fixed date. Printed copies, if any, must be delivered before it.
2. **Printing and delivery.** Ask your printer how long they need for your page count, quantity and binding, and allow for checking the printer's proof.
3. **Board or council approval.** The board usually approves a near-final version. Only agreed corrections should follow.
4. **Audit sign-off.** The audited financial statements cannot be finalised before this. Set them up from draft files earlier so only the figures change at the end.
5. **Proof rounds.** Agree how many rounds you will have and who consolidates the comments.
6. **Narrative copy deadlines.** Set a due date for each section and lay sections out as they are approved, rather than waiting for everything.
7. **Design system sign-off.** Cover, grid, type, chart and table styles, agreed on sample pages.
8. **Kick-off and flatplan.** A page-by-page plan of the report with page budgets and section owners, ideally agreed before financial year-end.

When the dates are written down in this order, the gaps become obvious, and so does the point at which a late section starts to threaten the board date.

## What to give your designer

To start, send:

- last year's report and anything you want to do differently;
- your brand guidelines, logo files and approved photographs;
- the key dates, from audit committee to publication;
- a single contact who can approve content and consolidate comments.

Then, as the year-end work progresses, approved copy section by section, data for charts in spreadsheets, and finally the financial statements as the Word and Excel files your finance team and auditors work from.

## Questions to ask before you start

- Who in our organisation owns each section, and who has final sign-off?
- Which dates are fixed, and which are we assuming?
- Are we printing, publishing online, or both? How many printed copies?
- Do we need an accessible PDF, and who will approve the alternative text?
- Will the financial statements be in the same document or separate?
- What do our auditors, company secretary or oversight department require us to include?

The last question is the one a designer cannot answer for you, and it is worth settling first. Everything else on this list is what we help you plan.
